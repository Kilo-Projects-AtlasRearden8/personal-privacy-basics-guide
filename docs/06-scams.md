# Spotting scams

Scams trick you into giving away money, passwords, or access to your devices. They arrive by email, text, phone call, social media message, or even a pop-up on your computer.

This chapter shows the common signs and what to do. Every example here is made up — do not treat any address, number, or name as real.

## Common types of scams

### Phishing (email)
A fake email that looks like it comes from your bank, a store, a delivery company, or the government. It says there is a problem and asks you to click a link or open an attachment.

### Smishing (text message)
Same idea as phishing, but by text. Often pretends to be a delivery delay, a missed payment, or a prize you won.

### Vishing (phone call)
Someone calls claiming to be from your bank, tech support, the IRS, or a relative in trouble. They pressure you to act fast.

### Tech support scams
A pop-up says your computer has a virus and gives a phone number to call. Or someone calls you saying they are "Microsoft" or "Apple" and found a problem. Real companies do not call you out of the blue about viruses.

### Romance and friendship scams
Someone you met online gains your trust over weeks or months, then asks for money, gift cards, or help moving money.

### Too-good-to-be-true offers
A job you never applied for, a prize you never entered, an investment with guaranteed high returns, or a luxury item at half price.

## Red flags to watch for

| Red flag | What it looks like |
|----------|-------------------|
| Urgency | "Act now or your account will be closed" |
| Threats | "You will be arrested" or "We are suing you" |
| Unexpected | You did not order anything, but they say you have a delivery |
| Strange sender | `support@paypa1-example.com` instead of a real company domain |
| Generic greeting | "Dear Customer" instead of your name |
| Bad spelling/grammar | Not always present — AI scams can look polished |
| Odd payment request | Gift cards, wire transfer, crypto, or payment apps |
| Request for passwords or 2FA codes | No real company asks for your password or verification code |
| Too good to be true | Free money, prizes you did not enter, guaranteed returns |

## How to check a link before clicking

1. Hover your mouse over the link (on a phone, press and hold). The real destination appears near the bottom of the screen or in a pop-up.
2. Look closely at the domain. `account-security-example.com` is not your bank's real site.
3. When in doubt, do not click. Go to the company's real website by typing the address yourself, or call them using a number from a paper statement or the back of your card — not a number in the message.

## What to do when you get a suspicious message

1. **Do not click links or open attachments.**
2. **Do not call phone numbers in the message.**
3. **Do not reply.** Replying confirms your number or email is active.
4. Check the real company yourself: log in to your account by typing the website address, or call using a trusted number.
5. Delete the message.
6. Report it:
   - Email: forward to your email provider's spam/report address (many providers have a "Report phishing" button).
   - Text: forward to 7726 (SPAM) in the US.
   - Government: report at reportfraud.ftc.gov (US) or your country's consumer protection agency.

## If you think you were scammed

1. **Stop paying.** If you sent money via gift card, wire, or crypto, it is usually gone — but report it anyway.
2. **Change passwords.** Change the password on any account you entered, and any other account where you used the same password.
3. **Turn on 2FA** if you have not already (see the accounts chapter).
4. **Contact your bank or card issuer** if you gave them financial information. Ask to freeze or replace the card.
5. **Place a fraud alert** on your credit (in the US, contact one of the three credit bureaus; they must notify the others).
6. **Report it** at reportfraud.ftc.gov (US) or your local equivalent.
7. **Tell someone.** Scams thrive on embarrassment. Telling a friend or family member helps you and helps them spot the same scam.

## Quick checklist for this chapter

- [ ] I do not click links in unexpected emails or texts
- [ ] I check the real destination of links by hovering
- [ ] I never give passwords or 2FA codes to anyone
- [ ] I do not pay with gift cards, wire transfer, or crypto on someone's say-so
- [ ] I verify urgent requests through a separate channel (the real website or a trusted phone number)
- [ ] I know how to report spam/phishing in my email and texts
- [ ] I know to report fraud at reportfraud.ftc.gov (US) or my local agency

If something feels off, it probably is. Slow down and check before you act.
